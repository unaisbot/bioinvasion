The science behind the spread

The story of biological invasion.

From arrival to establishment, spread and impact — explore how species move into new environments, reshape ecosystems and become a global conservation challenge.

01 · The concept

What is biological invasion?

Biological invasion is a process in which organisms are introduced, directly or indirectly, beyond their natural geographical range, establish populations and spread in a new region.

Not every species found outside its native range becomes invasive. Invasion science examines the conditions that allow some introduced species to establish, expand and cause ecological, economic or social impacts.

Remember: “Non-native” describes where a species occurs in relation to its natural range. “Invasive” refers to a subset of introduced species that spread and cause, or are likely to cause, adverse impacts.
02 · Know the difference

Native, non-native and invasive

These terms are related, but they do not mean the same thing.

01 / NATIVE

Native species

A species that occurs naturally in a region, having reached it through natural processes rather than human introduction.

02 / NON-NATIVE

Non-native species

A species occurring outside its natural geographical range because of intentional or unintentional human activities.

03 / INVASIVE ALIEN

Invasive alien species

An introduced species that establishes and spreads, causing or posing a risk of negative impacts on biodiversity, ecosystems or people.

03 · The invasion process

How does an invasion unfold?

Biological invasions are dynamic processes. A useful way to understand them is to follow the stages from introduction to impact.

Introduction

A species is transported beyond its natural range, intentionally or accidentally.

Establishment

The introduced organisms survive and reproduce, forming a self-sustaining population.

Spread

The population expands into new locations and may occupy larger areas.

Impact

The species causes ecological, economic or social harm in the invaded region.

The stages are a simplified educational model. In real ecosystems, invasion pathways and outcomes can be complex, and not every introduced species progresses through every stage.

04 · Pathways

How do species travel?

Human activities connect distant ecosystems. Species can move across borders through several intentional and unintentional pathways.

Trade and transport

Organisms may travel as contaminants or stowaways in cargo, ships, vehicles, packaging and transported materials.

Horticulture and pets

Plants and animals introduced for gardens, aquariums, pets or ornamental use may escape or be released into nature.

Agriculture and forestry

Species may be introduced for crops, pasture, forestry, biological control or other production-related purposes.

Waterways and shipping

Marine and freshwater organisms can be transported in ballast water, on vessel surfaces or through connected waterways.

Travel and tourism

Seeds, insects and other organisms can move unintentionally with travellers, luggage, equipment and outdoor goods.

Intentional release

Species may be deliberately released into the environment, sometimes without anticipating their future spread or impacts.

05 · Consequences

Why do biological invasions matter?

Impacts vary between species and ecosystems. They can affect biodiversity, ecosystem functioning and human well-being.

Biodiversity and native species

Invasive species may compete with native organisms, prey on them, spread pathogens or alter habitats.

Ecosystem processes

Invasions can change nutrient cycling, water availability, disturbance patterns and ecosystem structure.

Agriculture and production

Some invasive species affect crops, grazing lands, forestry, fisheries and other livelihood activities.

People and communities

Impacts may affect health, access to natural resources, recreation, cultural values and local economies.

06 · A global and local challenge

Biological invasions across scales

Invasions occur in terrestrial, freshwater and marine environments. Their causes and consequences are shaped by local ecological and social conditions.

A global perspective

Species introductions and spread are connected to global trade, travel, land-use change and other human activities. International cooperation and prevention are important because pathways cross political boundaries.

India and Kerala

India's varied landscapes include forests, grasslands, islands, rivers, lakes and wetlands. Kerala's diverse ecosystems make monitoring, documentation and context-specific management of invasive species especially important.

07 · Prevention and management

What can we do?

Preventing new introductions and detecting emerging invasions early are often more effective than trying to manage widespread established populations.

Prevention

Reduce risky introductions through pathway management, awareness, biosecurity and responsible trade and planting practices.

Early detection

Monitor ecosystems, document observations, verify identifications and report new occurrences promptly.

Management and restoration

Use context-appropriate control, long-term monitoring and restoration to support native biodiversity and ecosystem recovery.
